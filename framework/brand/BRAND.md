# Form & Signal — workspace identity

## Purpose

Form & Signal is the maker identity for this presentation workspace: a reminder to pair a clear message with a deliberate visual form. It is not a required logo or theme for user decks. Each presentation should get a visual identity suited to its subject and audience unless the author asks to use this one.

## Identity

- **Name:** Form & Signal
- **Position:** Original, research-aware presentation craft for ideas that need to be understood and remembered.
- **Promise:** Make the argument legible; make the visual choice earn its place.
- **Voice:** Direct, curious, specific, never salesy.
- **Design principles:** Strong hierarchy; purposeful color; varied composition; precise alignment; visual metaphors that explain; readable body copy; no default header or footer.

## Visual system

| Token | Value | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ink | `#101826` | Primary dark field and text |
| Paper | `#F5F0E6` | Warm light field |
| Signal coral | `#F36F4C` | Focus, annotation, key turn |
| Signal citron | `#C8F36A` | Secondary highlight and active line |
| Cobalt | `#5478EB` | Data and structural contrast |
| Muted slate | `#667085` | Supporting text on paper |

Use one dominant field and at most two signal colors on a page. Do not apply gradients as decoration. Use them only to show depth, direction, or material. Pair a sturdy sans-serif with an editorial serif only when the subject benefits from contrast. Use system fonts and verify the rendered output on the target machine.

## Mark

The mark combines a framed page corner with a signal path and a single point. It is original SVG geometry. Keep its proportions and clear space; do not place it on a user deck unless requested.

## Usage

This identity guides internal sample libraries, notes, and maker-facing previews. It must not compete with a client's or author's identity. The topic's design system takes priority in each presentation.

## Applying the identity

Treat the brand as a small system of decisions, not a logo pasted on every slide:

1. **Choose the message first.** Write the one-sentence purpose, audience, and intended tone before selecting a visual direction. The deck's subject may call for a different palette or image language.
2. **Keep the signature recognizable.** Use the mark, wordmark, palette, and voice together for workspace-facing materials. Preserve the SVG proportions and keep clear space around it equal to the mark's small coral dot. Never stretch, recolor arbitrarily, or place it over a busy photo.
3. **Use color by role.** Ink and paper carry most surfaces; cobalt organizes information; coral and citron call attention to a small number of moments. In charts, pair color with labels, symbols, or line styles. Color must not be the only cue.
4. **Set a readable type hierarchy.** Use a display face for short headlines and a highly legible sans-serif for body text. Keep body copy left aligned, line lengths controlled, and emphasis to one or two treatments. Break long headlines into deliberate lines instead of shrinking them to fit.
5. **Make imagery belong.** Use original, licensed, or author-provided imagery. Choose a consistent treatment for photos, illustrations, and diagrams, or make a deliberate contrast. Images should explain or set context; avoid unrelated stock-like portraits and decorative assets without a message.
6. **Apply the grid to create rhythm.** Align titles, body copy, diagrams, and captions to a shared margin/grid, while varying composition when the content benefits from it. Consistency comes from spacing, type, color roles, and image treatment—not from repeating one slide layout.
7. **Check the actual output.** Review each slide at presentation size and at 100%. Confirm no clipping or collisions, the reading order is clear, and small text has strong contrast. Target WCAG contrast of 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for large text; check text against the lightest or lowest-contrast part of any gradient.

### Presentation workflow

- Use Form & Signal as the visible identity only for the workspace's own guides, templates, and samples.
- For a user's deck, create a project-specific art direction from the subject, audience, and brief. Do not add the Form & Signal mark unless asked.
- Carry forward selected craft rules—clear hierarchy, disciplined alignment, purposeful color, and readable type—without carrying over the maker palette or logo by default.
- Review a cover as its own composition. Keep the title brief, ensure it reads before the supporting line, and make every image or graphic relevant to the topic.

## Research notes

These references informed the usage guidance; the identity and its visual assets remain original:

- [Google Design: Staying true to your identity with Material branding](https://design.google/library/staying-true-to-your-identity-material-branding) — a brand can customize color, typography, imagery, and iconography while preserving a coherent system; imagery should feel related or be intentionally varied.
- [IBM Design Language: Type basics](https://www.ibm.com/design/language/typography/type-basics/) — hierarchy, flush-left alignment, comfortable line lengths, deliberate line breaks, and restrained emphasis support legibility.
- [IBM Design Language: Color](https://www.ibm.com/design/language/color/) — build palettes with clear roles, use accent colors purposefully, verify contrast, and do not rely on color alone to communicate meaning.
- [IBM Design Language: Layout overview](https://www.ibm.com/design/language/layout/overview/) — composition expresses character through hierarchy, scale, proportion, contrast, harmony, and rhythm.
- [Canva: Brand guidelines](https://www.canva.com/docs/brand-guidelines/) — make guidance concrete with exact asset use, color values, typography rules, examples, and room for bounded creative choice.
